# Distributed Pathway Model (DPM)

    <h2>The Distributed Pathway Model: Mechanistic Foundation</h2>

    <p>The Distributed Pathway Model (DPM) is the mechanism I propose for the Higher-Resolution Hypothesis. All cortex is recurrent and divergent; what I suspect differs in autism is how tightly the alternative routes are gated. My working picture is that shifts in neural topology—more axonal and dendritic branching, and less precise inhibitory gating—could let signals that would normally stay within one stream spill across into functionally distinct networks. If that is right, it would produce higher-resolution processing through **ensemble permutations**: the same stimulus could recruit different overlapping ensembles (transient coalitions of neurons) depending on signal strength, phase, and context. The idea is not simply that more neurons fire, but that the **space of possible ensemble combinations** is larger—which I would expect to bring finer perceptual and cognitive distinctions alongside higher energetic cost, more temporal variability, and more vulnerability to cascades across functional boundaries. None of this is measured or modeled; it is a sketch of the moving parts.</p>

    <h3>Topological Foundation: Branching, Pruning, and Lifelong Collateral Formation</h3>

    <p>During typical neurodevelopment, initial exuberant synaptogenesis is followed by extensive synaptic pruning that eliminates redundant or weakly-activated connections, streamlining neural pathways. My reading of the literature is that in autism several mechanisms may contribute to increased branching. Reduced pruning could be one: some post-mortem work has reported higher spine density on cortical pyramidal neurons, and other studies report increased gray matter volume in childhood and elevated synaptic protein expression. I do not attribute the extra density solely to pruning, though—lifelong collateral formation and atypical stabilization of newly formed synapses may also contribute, extending distributed propagation beyond early development. If so, the result would be a topology where a single presynaptic input can activate multiple partially-overlapping postsynaptic ensembles rather than converging on a single dominant pathway. None of these findings was gathered to test the DPM; they are consistent with it, which is not the same as evidence for it.</p>

    <p>This branching would create representational redundancy and cross-network connections: the same stimulus could activate multiple partially distinct neural populations spanning different functional systems, each encoding slightly different features or contextual associations. At the circuit level, I would expect this to look like local hyperconnectivity—denser short-range connections within local modules—coexisting with cross-network connections that let signals bleed between systems that would otherwise stay separate. Many fMRI and DTI studies report increased short-range correlations and decreased long-range functional connectivity in autism. That is consistent with the DPM, though not specific to it: E/I-imbalance and other accounts would expect much the same pattern.</p>

    <h3>Inhibitory Gating: Timing and Strength</h3>

    <p>Distributed propagation is further amplified by altered inhibitory control. GABAergic interneurons, particularly parvalbumin-positive (PV+) and somatostatin-positive (SST+) populations, normally provide feedforward and feedback inhibition that constrains signal spread. In autism, several lines of work have been read as pointing toward a shift in E/I balance toward excitation: studies report reduced GABAergic signaling markers, altered GABA receptor expression, delayed maturation of inhibitory circuits, and, in some cortical regions, relatively more excitatory than inhibitory neurons. I am not sure whether inhibition is weaker overall or less precise in time—stronger in some places and weaker in others.</p>

    <p>Gating functions much like a **dike in a branching river**: low-energy inputs remain confined to the primary channel, while stronger or prolonged signals can overflow into neighboring routes. These latent collaterals—normally pruned or inhibited in neurotypical development—introduce *potential* cross-activation without guaranteeing it. This **conditional** architecture means that signals can activate one route, both routes, or oscillate between them depending on membrane potential, local field effects, or inhibitory timing.</p>

    <p>If inhibition is somewhat weaker or later than typical, I would expect distributed activation to persist longer: signals that would normally be quenched quickly could keep propagating, engaging additional downstream circuits before they are shut down. That would create temporal spreading—activation windows widen, which could make it harder to segregate sequential inputs and reduce temporal resolution even as spatial and featural resolution increases. I do not know how large any of these effects would be.</p>

    <h3>Oscillatory Consequences</h3>

    <p>Distributed branching with less precise inhibition would also be expected to change network oscillations. Electrophysiological studies have reported altered gamma oscillations in autism—often reduced power and coherence—which is usually interpreted as disrupted synchronization and is consistent with, though not specific to, altered PV+ interneuron function. Reduced gamma synchrony could impair local binding and temporal coordination. Beta oscillations, associated with top-down control, have also been reported as altered, and some studies report enhanced or prolonged alpha/theta activity, which might reflect compensatory mechanisms or alternative coordination strategies.</p>

    <p>My reading of these findings is that they could be signatures of distributed propagation: local synchrony where branches reinforce one another, and interregional desynchrony where inhibitory coordination fails. Instead of coherent oscillatory packets organizing information flow, multiple semi-autonomous populations would activate with variable phase relationships—reducing binding efficiency but allowing more independent representations to coexist, at the cost of coordination.</p>

    <h3>Predictive Coding Interpretation</h3>

    <p>Predictive-coding accounts such as HIPPEA propose that autistic perception gives high precision to sensory input relative to priors. The DPM offers one account of how structure could yield that: more local branches would dedicate more neurons to encoding incoming data, making bottom-up signals heavier relative to top-down compression, while reduced long-range coordination would limit the influence of priors. If so, the system would favor data-driven over model-driven processing—precise under consistent conditions, but harder to generalize under ambiguity. Predictive-coding accounts expect most of the same behavior without the DPM, so this is complementary rather than a distinguishing test.</p>

    <h3>Active vs. Reactive Pathways</h3>

    <p>Not all distributed pathways would operate identically. The DPM distinguishes between active pathways—those engaging executive control, working memory, and volitional action—and reactive pathways—those operating relatively autonomously (sensory-motor loops, emotional-associative circuits, memory reactivation). I would expect the balance between the two to differ from person to person and from region to region, which is one way I make sense of heterogeneity and "spiky" profiles; I have no idea what that balance actually is in anyone. Someone whose branching is more active-dominant might show strong executive function but heavier cognitive load; someone whose branching is more reactive-dominant might show exceptional perceptual abilities but more difficulty with volitional regulation.</p>

    <h3>Emergent Phenomenology</h3>

    <p>If the architecture is as I describe, this is how I would read many features commonly described in autism: detail perception (more ensemble combinations per stimulus), sensory overload (insufficient filtering), task-switching difficulty (persistent prior activation), decision paralysis (competing activations), pattern recognition (redundant encoding), predictability preference (minimizing prediction error across distributed pathways), special interests (islands of stability), stimming (reactive motor cascade), and energetic collapse (local energy demand outrunning supply). None of these is a finding; they are the readings that make the most sense to me if the model is right.</p>

    <p>Critically, DPM is not a deficit model. It describes a different neural architecture with distinct computational properties—one where functional boundaries between processing systems are more permeable. The same cross-network topology that would produce challenges (multi-domain interference, sensory cross-talk) under high load could also enable real strengths (integrative insights, synesthetic richness) under the right conditions. If the model is right, it would point toward accommodating cross-network processing rather than forcing functional isolation—though that is what I would try, not something that has been evaluated, and it is not clinical advice.</p>

    <h2>How Neural Architecture Shapes Information Processing</h2>

    <div style="text-align:center; margin: 2rem 0;">
      <img src="../../diagrams/dpm-comparison-side-by-side.svg" alt="The Distributed Pathway Model" style="max-width:500px; width:80%; height:auto;">
      <p style="margin-top: 1rem;"><em><strong>Figure. Neurotypical vs Distributed Pathway processing (ensembles).</strong> Nodes (A–Z) denote **ensembles** (transient coalitions of neurons). Solid blue/green edges show stable within-network routes; dashed red edges are **conditional collaterals** that can activate depending on input strength/phase/context. In DPM, these latent routes expand **ensemble permutations**, increasing perceptual granularity without implying constant cross-activation.</em></p>
    </div>

    <p>The Distributed Pathway Model (DPM) is my attempt to describe a neural architecture that could underlie autistic cognition. It proposes that autistic circuits may develop and operate differently in two ways: more branching connections, and weaker or less precisely timed inhibitory control that lets signals spread more broadly. It is a sketch, not something that has been measured or modeled.</p>

    <h3>Branching Topology: One Input, Multiple Routes</h3>

    <p>During typical neurodevelopment, initial abundant connections between neurons are streamlined through pruning, eliminating redundant or weakly-used pathways. This creates relatively direct routes where information follows predictable paths (A → B → C). In autism, my reading is that reduced pruning may be one contributing factor—some studies report higher spine density, increased gray matter volume persisting into adolescence, and elevated synaptic protein expression, though none of that work was designed to test this model. Ongoing collateral formation and atypical stabilization of new synapses throughout life may further increase branching, extending distributed propagation beyond early development.</p>

    <p>The result would be additional collateral branches at neural junctions. A single input could then activate multiple partially-overlapping downstream pathways rather than converging on one dominant route (A → B → C <em>and</em> A → B → Y → Z). That creates representational redundancy: the same stimulus activates multiple neural populations, each encoding slightly different features or associations.</p>

    <p>At the circuit level, I would expect this to look like local hyperconnectivity—denser short-range connections within local brain modules—alongside reduced coordination across distant regions. Many brain imaging studies report increased short-range correlations and decreased long-range functional connectivity in autism, which is consistent with this picture, though not specific to it.</p>

    <h3>Inhibitory Gating: Delayed or Weakened Signal Termination</h3>

    <p>Distributed propagation is amplified by altered inhibitory control. Inhibitory neurons normally act as brakes, constraining how far signals spread and how long they persist. In autism, several lines of work have been read as suggesting these brakes may operate more weakly or slowly: studies report reduced GABAergic signaling markers, altered inhibitory receptor expression, and delayed maturation of inhibitory circuits.</p>

    <p>If the brakes are weaker or later, distributed activation would persist longer: signals that would normally be quenched quickly could keep propagating, engaging additional downstream circuits before they stop. That would create temporal spreading—activation windows widen, which could make it harder to separate one input from the next even as spatial resolution increases.</p>

    <h3>Higher Resolution Through Ensemble Permutations</h3>

    <p>The idea is that this architecture would increase representational density not just by engaging more neurons, but by expanding the **space of possible ensemble combinations**. The same stimulus could recruit different overlapping groups of neurons (ensembles) depending on input strength, creating finer distinctions. This is how I would account for the subtle patterns, sensory distinctions, and details many autistic people notice that more compressed processing would miss—the brain would have access to more possible representational states.</p>

    <p>If so, this resolution would come with costs:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Energetic demand:</strong> More routes running for longer would cost more energy, paid locally where the activity happens</li>
      <li><strong>Temporal variability:</strong> Asynchronous pathways create coordination challenges</li>
      <li><strong>Cascade vulnerability:</strong> Weak inhibition allows activation to spread broadly</li>
      <li><strong>Filtering difficulty:</strong> More signals persist, making it harder to suppress irrelevant information</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Active vs. Reactive Pathways</h3>

    <p>Not all distributed pathways would operate identically. DPM distinguishes between active pathways (executive control, working memory, volitional action) and reactive pathways (sensory-motor loops, emotional-associative circuits, autonomous processing). I would expect the balance to differ across individuals and brain regions, which is one way to make sense of how differently autism presents. More active branching might enhance executive function but increase cognitive load; more reactive branching might enhance perception but reduce volitional control.</p>

    <h3>Not Deficit, But Different</h3>

    <p>DPM is not a deficit model—it describes a different neural architecture with distinct computational properties. If it is right, the same distributed topology that produces challenges under high load would also support real strengths under the right conditions: detail perception, pattern recognition, and sensory acuity would come from the same branching structure that creates overload vulnerability and filtering difficulty.</p>

    <p>If the model is right, it would point toward accommodating distributed processing rather than forcing hierarchical compression. What I would try—reduced concurrent demands, predictable patterns, sensory accommodations—might let the strengths of high-resolution processing show without the costs of constant overload. None of this has been evaluated, and it is not clinical advice.</p>

    <h2>The Core Difference: How Your Brain Is Wired</h2>

    <div style="text-align:center; margin: 2rem 0;">
      <img src="../../diagrams/dpm-comparison-side-by-side.svg" alt="The Distributed Pathway Model" style="max-width:500px; width:80%; height:auto;">
      <p style="margin-top: 1rem;"><em><strong>The distributed pathway model.</strong> Information can flow through multiple possible combinations of neuron groups (ensembles) depending on signal strength. This creates many more possible processing patterns—richer detail—but requires more energy and coordination.</em></p>
    </div>

    <p>The Distributed Pathway Model is my best guess at how the autistic brain might be wired differently from neurotypical brains—and why that could bring both real strengths and genuine challenges. It's an idea, not an established finding, but it's the picture that makes the most sense to me.</p>

    <h3>More Pathways: Reduced Pruning Plus Ongoing Growth</h3>

    <p>Think of your brain like a city's road network. When the city is first built, there are roads everywhere—many possible routes to get from any place to any other place. As the city develops, urban planners remove redundant roads to make traffic flow more efficiently, keeping only the most useful routes.</p>

    <p>In typical brain development, this "pruning" happens extensively. The baby brain starts with an enormous number of connections between neurons, then eliminates many of them during childhood and adolescence, streamlining pathways. By adulthood, information tends to flow along well-established, efficient routes.</p>

    <p>In autistic brain development, I think two things may be happening: First, this pruning may be less aggressive—more of those extra connections are kept. Second, and importantly, the brain may keep building new side roads throughout life, with new connections being kept in ways that happen less in typical brains. If so, when information enters your brain, instead of flowing down one main highway, it can flow down multiple interconnected roads at once—and those roads keep expanding over time, not just in childhood.</p>

    <p><strong>Example:</strong> A sound enters your ear. In a neurotypical brain, the signal might follow one primary pathway to be processed and categorized. In your brain, that same sound might activate multiple pathways simultaneously—one analyzing its pitch, another its timbre, another its emotional associations, another comparing it to similar sounds you've heard before, all happening at once through preserved neural connections.</p>

    <h3>Cross-Network Spillover: The Key Insight</h3>

    <p>Think of your brain as having many specialized departments: visual processing, sound processing, movement planning, emotional responses. In most brains, these departments work independently—a signal in the visual department stays in the visual department. In autistic brains, my guess is that more of the connections between departments were kept during development. Then when the visual department activates, signals can spill over into the sound department, the emotion department, or the movement department. That would help explain why a bright light (visual) might trigger sound sensitivity, emotional reactions, or the urge to move (stimming). It's not that each system is broken—it's that the boundaries between systems may be more permeable.</p>

    <p>This is how I would make sense of the way autism shows up across so many areas at once: activation in one system (e.g., visual processing) can end up recruiting circuits in another (e.g., emotional processing, motor planning, or hearing). If the model is right, these aren't separate symptoms—they come from the same underlying spillover between networks.</p>

    <h3>Why This Creates "Higher Resolution"</h3>

    <p>If your brain can activate different **combinations** of neuron groups (ensembles) depending on signal strength, you wouldn't just be capturing more raw data—you'd be accessing more possible **representational states**. Think of it like having more settings on a camera: the same scene can be captured in more different ways, revealing details that a simpler camera with fewer settings would miss.</p>

    <p>It's like the difference between a standard photo and a high-resolution photo:</p>

    <p><strong>Standard photo:</strong> Captures the main image clearly, good enough for most purposes, processes quickly<br>
    <strong>High-resolution photo:</strong> Captures extraordinary detail invisible in the standard photo, but creates much larger file sizes, takes longer to process, requires more storage and computing power</p>

    <p>In this picture, your brain is the high-resolution camera, with more possible combinations of active neuron groups. That would be why you notice details others miss—subtle patterns, minute changes, fine distinctions—because you have more possible ways of representing the same information.</p>

    <h3>The Brake System: Inhibitory Control</h3>

    <p>Now imagine those multiple pathways the information is flowing through. In typical brains, there's a strong "brake system"—inhibitory neurons that quickly stop signals from spreading too far or lasting too long. These brakes keep activation contained.</p>

    <p>In autistic brains, I suspect these brakes may engage more slowly or less strongly. If so:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Signals spread to more areas than they would in typical brains</li>
      <li>Activation lasts longer before being shut down</li>
      <li>Information keeps processing when it would have already stopped in typical brains</li>
    </ul>

    <p>That would amplify the distributed processing—not only would you have more pathways, but signals flow through them longer and spread further.</p>

    <h3>The Trade-offs</h3>

    <p><strong>What this distributed architecture enables:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Noticing subtle details others miss (more neurons capturing more dimensions)</li>
      <li>Detecting patterns in complex data (redundant encoding across pathways)</li>
      <li>Exceptional accuracy when patterns are consistent (high-resolution detection)</li>
      <li>Deep, systematic understanding of areas of interest (extensive neural encoding)</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>What this distributed architecture makes challenging:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Filtering out irrelevant information (more signals persist, harder to suppress)</li>
      <li>Switching between tasks (need to deactivate many pathways, not just one)</li>
      <li>Managing concurrent demands (multiple pathways active simultaneously)</li>
      <li>Energy management (more pathways running for longer would use more fuel)</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Why People Experience Autism So Differently</h3>

    <p>The distributed pathways probably don't develop identically in everyone's brain. I find it useful to think about two types:</p>

    <p><strong>Active pathways:</strong> Involve conscious control, decision-making, working memory, volitional action—the parts you control deliberately</p>

    <p><strong>Reactive pathways:</strong> Operate more automatically—sensory processing, emotional responses, motor patterns, memory associations</p>

    <p>Different people have different balances:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>More active branching:</strong> May have strong executive function and working memory but experience high cognitive load and mental exhaustion</li>
      <li><strong>More reactive branching:</strong> May have exceptional sensory and perceptual abilities but more difficulty with voluntary control and regulation</li>
    </ul>

    <p>This balance also varies by brain region—someone might have extensive distributed pathways in sensory areas (heightened sensory perception) but less in motor areas, or vice versa. This is one way to make sense of why autism looks so different across individuals—the distributed architecture would express differently depending on where and how extensively the extra pathways were kept.</p>

    <h3>Not Broken, Different</h3>

    <p>This distributed architecture isn't a malfunction or damage—it's a different way of being wired with different strengths and costs.</p>

    <p>A highway system with fewer, larger roads (typical brain):</p>
    <ul>
      <li>+ Traffic flows efficiently in predictable patterns</li>
      <li>+ Easy to manage and redirect</li>
      <li>- Captures less local detail</li>
      <li>- Misses subtle variations</li>
    </ul>

    <p>A road network with many interconnected smaller roads (autistic brain):</p>
    <ul>
      <li>+ Captures rich local detail</li>
      <li>+ Notices patterns others miss</li>
      <li>- Requires more energy to operate</li>
      <li>- Harder to coordinate efficiently</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Neither is better or worse—they're optimized for different things. If the model is right, the autistic brain would be well suited to high-resolution processing, pattern detection, and detail capture, but would need more energy and more supportive environments to work well. Things like predictable patterns, fewer demands at once, and sensory accommodations are what I would try to let those strengths show without constant overload—though they haven't been tested as ways of supporting this architecture, and none of this is medical advice.</p>

    <h3>The Foundation for Everything Else</h3>

    <p>If this picture is right, it is the foundation the rest of this framework builds on. Here is how I would read other features of autism through it:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Why sensory experiences are more intense (more neurons processing each stimulus)</li>
      <li>Why routine and predictability matter (minimizing prediction errors across many pathways)</li>
      <li>Why tasks feel more complex (decompose into more steps through distributed processing)</li>
      <li>Why social situations are overwhelming (too many signals processed simultaneously)</li>
      <li>Why energy runs out (higher metabolic cost of distributed activation)</li>
      <li>Why stimming occurs (reactive motor cascade when distributed activation spills into motor circuits)</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Understanding your brain through this lens isn't about fixing what's broken—it's about understanding how your neural architecture works so you can work with it rather than against it.</p>
