    <h2>Individual Variability Through Active:Reactive Ratios</h2>

    <p>Autism exhibits extraordinary phenotypic heterogeneity despite shared genetic risk factors and common architectural features. The way I would account for this variability within the Distributed Pathway Model is through <strong>active versus reactive pathways</strong> and their regional distribution. Active pathways are directly driven by external stimuli or internal intentions—the "on-path" routes engaging executive control and goal-directed processing. Reactive pathways become engaged secondarily via distributed propagation—spillover activity that crosses collateral branches when inhibitory gating fails to contain activation.</p>

    <h3>Three Sources of Variability</h3>

    <p>In DPM terms, I would expect individual differences to come from person-specific differences in three things. These are shorthand for what would have to differ, not measured model parameters:</p>

    <ol>
      <li><strong>Branch density near critical nodes:</strong> How many potential reactive routes exist at key circuit junctions. Higher density would create more opportunities for spillover, increasing the reactive component of processing.</li>
      <li><strong>Inhibitory gating strength and timing:</strong> How tightly spillover is contained and how rapidly inhibition engages. Stronger, faster inhibition would limit reactive spread; weaker, delayed inhibition would allow broader reactive recruitment.</li>
      <li><strong>Temporal persistence of reactive activity:</strong> How long reactive pathways remain active once engaged. Longer persistence would create sustained reverberatory loops; shorter persistence would allow quicker return to baseline.</li>
    </ol>

    <p>Together, these would shape each individual's <strong>active:reactive ratio</strong>—the relative balance between goal-directed processing and spillover-driven activation. I use the ratio as a way of thinking about balance; I have no idea what it actually is in anyone.</p>

    <h3>Phenotypic Implications of Active:Reactive Ratios</h3>

    <p>I would expect a high active:reactive ratio (strong inhibition, limited spillover, short persistence) to produce high-resolution processing with relatively stable self-regulation. Detail perception and pattern recognition would remain strong, but cascade vulnerability would be lower. Executive control would function more effectively because fewer competing reactive pathways interfere with goal-directed processing. Sensory overload thresholds would be higher, task-switching less costly, and energetic demands more manageable. These individuals may present as highly functional with less visible support needs, though they still experience higher cognitive load than neurotypical individuals.</p>

    <p>A low active:reactive ratio (weak inhibition, extensive spillover, prolonged persistence) would yield the same high-resolution encoding but with greater overlap, reverberation, and regulatory volatility. Reactive pathways would dominate processing, creating autonomous loops that operate relatively independently of volitional control. I would expect strong perceptual abilities and pattern recognition alongside increased vulnerability to sensory overload, emotional dysregulation, cascade activation, stimming frequency, and energetic collapse, with support needs that are likely to be more visible and extensive.</p>

    <h3>Domain-Specific Expression</h3>

    <p>Active:reactive ratios need not be uniform across brain regions. I would also expect <strong>domain-specific variability</strong>: an individual might have extensive reactive branching in primary sensory cortices (producing sensory overload) but tighter gating in language networks (enabling fluent verbal processing). Another might show the reverse pattern: well-regulated sensory processing coexisting with highly distributed language networks (producing echolalia, pragmatic challenges, or verbal perseveration).</p>

    <p>This kind of regional heterogeneity is consistent with the "spiky" profiles common in autism: islands of exceptional ability alongside areas of significant challenge. As illustrations only: Visual-spatial processing might operate with high active control (enabling exceptional pattern recognition) while social-emotional processing operates with high reactive spread (producing difficulty modulating affective responses). Mathematical reasoning might show tight gating (systematic, rule-based excellence) while motor planning shows extensive reactive loops (dyspraxia, stimming).</p>

    <h3>Genetic Risk and Phenotypic Diversity</h3>

    <p>This mapping is one way to make sense of why individuals with similar genetic risk factors can present very differently. Genetic variants may influence the probability of branching formation, pruning selectivity, and inhibitory strength—but the specific developmental trajectory would determine <em>where</em> branches form, <em>which</em> circuits develop tighter versus looser gating, and <em>how long</em> reactive activity persists. On this view, the pattern and persistence of reactive spread—not just the presence of branching—would shape day-to-day experience and support needs, and even monozygotic twins could differ through developmental variation in reactive topology.</p>

    <p>As a hypothetical: two siblings carrying the same CHD8 haploinsufficiency might both develop distributed architecture, but if one developed stronger PV+ interneuron networks in prefrontal cortex while the other developed them preferentially in sensory cortex, I would expect their phenotypes to diverge despite shared genetic etiology.</p>

    <h3>What This Might Mean in Practice</h3>

    <p>If the model were right, thinking about variability through active:reactive mappings would shift attention from categorical labels toward each person's particular profile. What I would try is to match support to where a person's reactive spread seems greatest rather than applying generic protocols: sensory accommodations where sensory spread dominates, affect regulation support where emotional circuits seem most reactive, and cognitive load reduction for more active-dominated profiles. None of this has been evaluated, and it is not clinical advice.</p>

    <p>I do not know how anyone would measure an active:reactive balance in a living brain, region by region. For what would make me more or less confident in this picture, and what I don't know how to test, see <a href="../hrh-paper#8-what-would-change-my-mind-and-what-i-dont-know-how-to-test">What would change my mind, and what I don't know how to test</a> in the paper.</p>

    <h2>Why Autism Manifests So Differently Across Individuals</h2>

    <p>Autism is extraordinarily heterogeneous: individuals vary widely in sensory sensitivity, verbal ability, cognitive style, and support needs. Within the Distributed Pathway Model, I would account for this variability through the ratio and regional distribution of <strong>active versus reactive pathways</strong>. Active pathways are directly engaged by stimuli or intentions; reactive pathways are recruited secondarily through distributed propagation—spillover activity crossing from one branch to another when inhibition fails to contain it.</p>

    <h3>Three Sources of Individual Variation</h3>

    <p>I would expect individual differences to come from three things (shorthand for what would differ, not measured parameters):</p>

    <ol>
      <li><strong>Branch density:</strong> How many potential reactive routes exist near critical circuit nodes. Higher density would create more spillover opportunities.</li>
      <li><strong>Inhibitory gating strength:</strong> How tightly spillover is contained and how rapidly inhibition engages. Stronger inhibition would limit reactive spread.</li>
      <li><strong>Temporal persistence:</strong> How long reactive activity continues once triggered. Longer persistence would create sustained loops.</li>
    </ol>

    <p>Together, these would shape each person's <strong>active:reactive ratio</strong>—the balance between goal-directed processing and spillover-driven activation. It is a way of thinking about balance, not a measured quantity.</p>

    <h2>Why Every Autistic Person Is Different</h2>

    <p>One of the most striking features of autism is how differently it manifests across individuals. Two autistic people can have completely different profiles—one might be highly verbal with intense sensory sensitivities, while another is minimally verbal with exceptional visual-spatial skills but manageable sensory experiences. My guess is that this isn't because they have different "types" of autism, but because the distributed pathway architecture develops differently in each person's brain.</p>

    <h3>Active Pathways vs. Reactive Pathways</h3>

    <p>Think of your brain's pathways in two categories:</p>

    <p><strong>Active pathways:</strong> These are the routes you control deliberately—when you decide to focus on something, plan an action, or think through a problem. They're goal-directed, volitional processing.</p>

    <p><strong>Reactive pathways:</strong> These are routes that activate automatically when neural signals "spill over" from active pathways because the brake system (inhibitory neurons) doesn't contain them fully. They operate more autonomously—you don't directly control them, they just activate when signals spread.</p>

    <p>Everyone has both types, but I think the balance between them varies a lot. Some people may have stronger brakes (more active, less reactive). Others may have weaker brakes (less active, more reactive). In this model, that balance—the active:reactive ratio—would shape how autism shows up. Nobody has measured it; it's a way of thinking, not a number.</p>

    <h3>Different Ratios, Different Experiences</h3>

    <p><strong>High active:reactive ratio (stronger brakes, less spillover):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>High-resolution processing is still present (noticing details others miss)</li>
      <li>But cascade vulnerability is lower (less likely to overwhelm)</li>
      <li>Executive function works more reliably (better able to plan and organize)</li>
      <li>Sensory overload threshold is higher (can handle more input before collapse)</li>
      <li>Energy management is easier (though still harder than neurotypical)</li>
      <li>May appear "high-functioning" with less visible support needs</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Low active:reactive ratio (weaker brakes, more spillover):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Same high-resolution processing (detail perception is still strong)</li>
      <li>But reactive pathways dominate (more autonomous loops)</li>
      <li>Sensory overload happens more easily (signals spread broadly)</li>
      <li>Emotional regulation is harder (feelings cascade through circuits)</li>
      <li>Stimming is more frequent (reactive motor loops activate readily)</li>
      <li>Energy depletes faster (more concurrent activation)</li>
      <li>Support needs are more visible and extensive</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Important:</strong> These are what I would expect if the model is right, not established findings. And neither ratio is "better" or "worse"—they're different balances with different strengths and costs. Both would involve the same core architecture (distributed pathways), just with different levels of inhibitory control.</p>

    <h3>Different Ratios in Different Brain Areas: Spiky Profiles</h3>

    <p>Here's where it gets really interesting: I'd expect the active:reactive ratio not to be the same across your whole brain. Different regions could have different ratios, which is one way to make sense of the "spiky" profiles common in autism. The two profiles below are made-up illustrations:</p>

    <p><strong>Example Profile 1:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Sensory areas:</strong> Low active:reactive (weak brakes) → Sensory overload, hypersensitivity</li>
      <li><strong>Language areas:</strong> High active:reactive (strong brakes) → Fluent verbal ability, good language control</li>
      <li><strong>Visual-spatial areas:</strong> High active:reactive → Exceptional pattern recognition, strong visual processing</li>
      <li><strong>Social-emotional areas:</strong> Low active:reactive → Difficulty reading emotions, social exhaustion</li>
    </ul>

    <p>This person is verbally fluent and visually talented but struggles with sensory environments and social situations.</p>

    <p><strong>Example Profile 2:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Sensory areas:</strong> High active:reactive (strong brakes) → Well-regulated sensory processing</li>
      <li><strong>Language areas:</strong> Low active:reactive (weak brakes) → Echolalia, scripting, difficulty with pragmatics</li>
      <li><strong>Mathematical reasoning:</strong> High active:reactive → Systematic, rule-based excellence</li>
      <li><strong>Motor planning:</strong> Low active:reactive → Dyspraxia, extensive stimming</li>
    </ul>

    <p>This person handles sensory input well and excels at math but struggles with language flexibility and motor coordination.</p>

    <p>Regional differences like these would help account for why autism profiles are so variable and why someone can be exceptionally talented in one area while needing significant support in another.</p>

    <h3>Why Siblings Can Be So Different</h3>

    <p>You might wonder: if autism is genetic, why do siblings with the same genes present so differently?</p>

    <p>My answer is that genes may influence the <em>tendency</em> toward distributed architecture—they could affect the probability that extra pathways will be preserved and brakes will be weaker. But they wouldn't dictate exactly <em>where</em> in the brain these changes occur or <em>how strong</em> the brakes are in each region.</p>

    <p>During brain development, many factors could influence the specific pattern:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Which specific neurons form extra connections</li>
      <li>Where inhibitory neurons migrate and settle</li>
      <li>How strong the brakes develop in each region</li>
      <li>Random developmental variation (even identical twins differ)</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Two siblings might both have genes that create distributed architecture, but one develops stronger brakes in language areas while the other develops stronger brakes in sensory areas. Same genetic starting point, different developmental outcomes, very different presentations.</p>

    <h3>Why This Matters For Support</h3>

    <p>If this way of thinking is right, it would suggest tailoring support to each person's pattern rather than using generic "autism interventions." What follows is what I would try; none of it has been evaluated, and it isn't clinical advice:</p>

    <p><strong>High reactive spread in sensory areas?</strong> → Sensory accommodations, reduced sensory load, quiet spaces</p>

    <p><strong>High reactive spread in emotional circuits?</strong> → Affect regulation support, emotional processing help, understanding of emotional intensity</p>

    <p><strong>High reactive spread in motor areas?</strong> → Allow stimming, provide movement breaks, accommodate motor planning difficulties</p>

    <p><strong>High active control in specific domains?</strong> → Leverage those strengths, provide opportunities to engage exceptional abilities</p>

    <p>Generic approaches assume all autistic people have the same needs. An individualized approach would recognize that the same core architecture (distributed pathways) may express differently depending on where reactive spread is strongest and where active control is most effective.</p>

    <h3>The Big Picture</h3>

    <p>My sense is that autism's variability isn't random chaos—it may reflect how distributed architecture develops across different brain regions. If so, the active:reactive ratio and its regional distribution would shape:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Which sensory modalities are most sensitive</li>
      <li>Where executive function is strongest or weakest</li>
      <li>Which cognitive domains show exceptional ability</li>
      <li>What triggers overload most easily</li>
      <li>How visible support needs appear externally</li>
      <li>Which accommodations are likely to help most</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Thinking about your own pattern—where your brakes seem stronger or weaker, where reactive spread seems greatest—may help make sense of your particular combination of strengths and challenges, and may give you ideas about what support to try. It's a lens, not a diagnosis.</p>
