    <h2>Stimming and Reactive Motor Loops</h2>

    <p>Stimming—self-stimulatory repetitive movements such as hand-flapping, rocking, finger-tapping, pacing, or object manipulation—has historically been framed as maladaptive behavior requiring suppression. The Distributed Pathway Model suggests a different reading: stimming may originate as a reactive motor cascade when distributed activation exceeds inhibitory containment and spills into motor regions. In this framework, stimming is modeled as reactive output—an involuntary consequence of distributed propagation—rather than as an instrument of regulation.</p>

    <h3>Reactive Motor Cascade</h3>

    <p>In distributed architecture, concurrent activation across sensory, emotional, and cognitive circuits can exceed the capacity of inhibitory systems to contain it. When activation propagates through weakly gated branches connecting associative and emotional circuits to premotor and motor cortex, my reading is that the overflow shows up as spontaneous, patterned movement—not volitional motor planning, but reactive propagation finding the path of least resistance when internal gating fails to suppress spread.</p>

    <p>One reason the movements might be rhythmic and repetitive is that motor circuits tend to form oscillatory patterns when driven by sustained input. Once initiated, these patterns could persist through recurrent motor-sensory feedback loops: the movement generates proprioceptive and tactile feedback, which reactivates motor pathways, sustaining the cycle. I think of this as loosely analogous to central pattern generators in locomotion, but driven by overflow from cognitive-emotional load rather than locomotor intent. Repetition would continue as long as reactive propagation stays above threshold.</p>

    <p>Some studies report heightened motor cortex excitability and altered sensorimotor oscillations in autism, and many people observe that stimming increases with cognitive load, arousal, and sensory overload. None of this was gathered to test the DPM. It is consistent with stimming as an outlet for activation that cannot be internally gated, but it is also consistent with E/I-imbalance and predictive-coding accounts, so I do not treat it as evidence for this model specifically.</p>

    <h3>Suppression Costs and Phenomenological Effects</h3>

    <p>Some individuals report that stimming feels subjectively calming or helps restore focus. However, these reports are phenomenological—subjective experiences that may co-occur with stimming—and mechanistic regulation is out of scope in this model. The DPM does not model stimming as producing stabilization or entrainment effects; rather, stimming emerges when distributed activity spills into motor circuits (reactive cascade).</p>

    <p>What I would expect, if the model is right, is that forcibly suppressing reactive motor cascades carries real costs. Top-down suppression of emergent movement would recruit additional control networks (prefrontal cortex) and, under social threat, amygdala-mediated inhibitory drive, raising local energy demand and potentially destabilizing processing. Activation that would have become movement would remain distributed across internal circuits, adding to inhibitory and energetic load (see <a href="../intro/4-dual-constraints">Dual-Constraint section</a> and <a href="energetic-collapse">Energetic Constraints</a>).</p>

    <p>This is how I would account for the common report that forced suppression of stimming increases distress rather than reducing it: the system recruits costly control mechanisms while retaining the distributed activation that triggered the cascade in the first place. The energetic and inhibitory costs of suppression may worsen dysregulation by engaging additional networks under already-high load conditions.</p>

    <h3>Complex Outputs from Reactive Pathways</h3>

    <p>Reactive pathways are not limited to motor output. When distributed propagation forms closed loops across auditory imagery, motor simulation, and associative regions, I think the result can be autonomous oscillators—persistent internal experiences that run without volitional control. These would include involuntary music loops (earworms that can persist for hours or days), recursive thought patterns, spontaneous problem-solving that continues "in the background," and intrusive conceptual loops.</p>

    <p>My reading is that these phenomena reflect the network settling into stable dynamic patterns within its own topology. When activation propagating through distributed branches encounters a configuration that feeds back onto itself—where output from one circuit becomes input to another, which then reactivates the first—a self-sustaining loop forms. Whether this loop produces creative insight, autonomous problem-solving, or intrusive perseveration depends on its stability characteristics, informational content, and degree of inhibitory modulation.</p>

    <p>In favorable cases, these loops may support real strengths: mental music composition, problem-solving that seems to happen on its own, creative pattern recognition emerging from persistent background processing. On this reading, the same mechanism that produces intrusive loops can generate genuine insight when the loop engages content-rich associative pathways and remains open to external modulation. Conversely, when loops become rigid or engage circuits with high negative valence, they manifest as rumination, obsessive thought patterns, or perseveration.</p>

    <h3>Functional Role in Distributed Architecture</h3>

    <p>If the DPM is right, stimming and reactive loops are intrinsic features of distributed architecture under load, not dysfunctions to be eliminated. When distributed activation exceeds inhibitory containment, reactive cascades would emerge through the path of least resistance—often motor circuits. Suppressing these cascades without addressing the underlying distributed activation would leave the system managing overload through routes more likely to lead to collapse: sustained executive suppression (metabolically expensive and recruiting PFC control networks), amygdala-mediated fear/suppression (when social threat drives inhibition of natural output), or shutdown (when no outlet remains and energy reserves deplete).</p>

    <p>Understanding stimming as reactive motor cascade—rather than as a regulatory mechanism—points toward accommodation rather than suppression: recognizing stimming as a consequence of distributed propagation under load, providing environments where it can occur without social penalty, and reducing the sources of overload (sensory input, concurrent demands, prediction errors) that trigger cascades in the first place. On this model, suppression adds costs without addressing causes. This is what I would try if the model were right; none of it has been evaluated, and it is not clinical advice.</p>

    <h2>How Stimming Emerges as Reactive Cascade</h2>

    <h2>Why Stimming Happens</h2>

    <p>Stimming—repetitive movements like hand-flapping, rocking, finger-tapping, pacing, or fidgeting with objects—is one of the most visible features of autism. It's often viewed as something that needs to be stopped or hidden, but the way I understand it, stimming is a natural consequence of how the autistic brain processes information under load.</p>

    <h3>When the Brain Overflows Into Movement</h3>

    <p>Imagine a busy highway system where traffic is flowing through many interconnected routes. When traffic volume exceeds the system's capacity, cars start overflowing onto side roads, alternate routes, and eventually any available path. The overflow isn't intentional—it's just where traffic naturally goes when the main routes can't contain it all.</p>

    <p>My guess is that stimming works similarly. When your brain is processing a lot of sensory input, emotions, or thoughts simultaneously, all those neural signals create activation that spreads through many pathways. When this activation exceeds what your brain's "gating" systems can contain, it overflows into motor regions—the parts controlling movement. The result is spontaneous, repetitive motion.</p>

    <p>This would explain why stimming tends to increase when:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>You're overwhelmed:</strong> More sensory input or emotional intensity means more neural activation seeking outlets</li>
      <li><strong>You're excited:</strong> Positive emotions create activation that also needs somewhere to go</li>
      <li><strong>You're concentrating hard:</strong> Intense cognitive effort fills neural pathways, and movement provides an outlet</li>
      <li><strong>You're anxious or uncertain:</strong> Emotional distress creates sustained activation without clear resolution</li>
    </ul>

    <p>The movements aren't consciously chosen—they emerge naturally when your brain has more activation than it can internally manage. This is why trying to consciously stop stimming feels effortful and why it often returns as soon as attention shifts elsewhere.</p>

    <h3>Why Some People Feel Calmer When Stimming</h3>

    <p>Many people report that stimming feels calming, helps them focus, or makes overwhelming situations more manageable. These are real, valid experiences. However, from the model's perspective, these are phenomenological—things people experience and feel—rather than something this model tries to explain.</p>

    <p>What the model does offer is a way to think about what happens when stimming is suppressed:</p>

    <p><strong>When stimming is allowed:</strong> The overflow of activation finds an outlet through movement. The brain isn't trying to manage that activation internally anymore—it's expressing it.</p>

    <p><strong>When stimming is suppressed:</strong> That same activation is still there, but now you have to actively work to keep it from becoming movement. This requires:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>More brain resources:</strong> Your prefrontal cortex (control center) has to constantly monitor and suppress the movement urge</li>
      <li><strong>More energy:</strong> Suppression burns additional metabolic fuel on top of the already-high processing load</li>
      <li><strong>More stress:</strong> When suppression is driven by social fear ("I'll be judged if I stim"), your amygdala activates, adding emotional load</li>
    </ul>

    <p>This is why being told "stop doing that" feels immediately distressing, why forced suppression often increases anxiety, and why people often stim more intensely after being prevented from stimming—the activation builds up with nowhere to go, and suppressing it adds costs rather than solving the underlying overflow.</p>

    <h3>Songs Stuck in Your Head and Thought Loops</h3>

    <p>The same mechanism that creates stimming can also create internal loops—patterns that run in your mind without conscious control:</p>

    <p><strong>Music loops:</strong> A song gets "stuck in your head" and plays repeatedly for hours or days. My guess is that this happens when neural patterns for that music form a feedback loop—hearing it in your imagination reactivates the pathways that generate it, which makes you "hear" it again, which reactivates the pathways, and so on.</p>

    <p><strong>Thought loops:</strong> A particular thought pattern, problem, or conversation replay keeps running. You might mentally rehearse a conversation repeatedly, work on a problem in the background of your mind for days, or find certain ideas circling continuously.</p>

    <p><strong>Problem-solving that continues on its own:</strong> You think about a complex problem, then move on to other activities—but hours later, a solution suddenly appears. Your brain may have been still processing it in the background through sustained neural loops.</p>

    <p>Whether these loops are helpful or distressing depends on their content and stability:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Helpful loops:</strong> Working through creative problems, composing music mentally, developing complex ideas, recognizing patterns</li>
      <li><strong>Distressing loops:</strong> Ruminating on negative events, intrusive worries that won't resolve, obsessive thoughts, replaying mistakes</li>
    </ul>

    <p>On this model, these aren't separate from stimming—they're the same kind of mechanism (sustained feedback loops) happening internally instead of through movement.</p>

    <h3>Why Stopping Stimming Makes Things Worse</h3>

    <p>When autistic people are told to "stop stimming," "sit still," or "keep your hands quiet," it forces the brain to suppress a natural reactive output. This suppression itself has costs, and the underlying activation that triggered the cascade remains. Combined, this means:</p>

    <ul>
      <li><strong>Internal distress increases:</strong> The activation that would have overflowed into movement is still there, plus you're now spending resources suppressing the urge to move</li>
      <li><strong>Focus becomes harder:</strong> You're now allocating attention and control resources to movement suppression instead of the task at hand</li>
      <li><strong>Overload happens faster:</strong> The brain reaches its limits sooner because suppression adds costs (PFC control, amygdala activation under social threat) on top of existing load</li>
      <li><strong>Shutdown or meltdown becomes more likely:</strong> The combined energetic and inhibitory costs of suppression plus retained activation may bring collapse closer</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Many autistic adults report that being allowed to stim freely makes a big difference to how well they function. My reading is that it's not that stimming itself accomplishes tasks—it's that not suppressing stimming avoids the additional costs (control networks, energy, stress) that make overload worse and tasks impossible.</p>

    <h3>The Core Understanding</h3>

    <p>Stimming is not:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>A bad habit to break</li>
      <li>Purposeless behavior</li>
      <li>Something to be ashamed of</li>
      <li>A sign of lack of control</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Stimming is:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>The brain's natural response to activation overflow</li>
      <li>A reactive motor cascade—involuntary output when distributed activation exceeds containment</li>
      <li>An outlet for activation that has nowhere else to go</li>
      <li>An intrinsic feature of how distributed architecture responds to high information load</li>
    </ul>

    <p>If this is right, it reframes how we think about stimming. Instead of trying to suppress it (which, on this model, increases distress and cognitive load by recruiting additional control networks and energy), support would look more like the list below. These are what I would try, not evaluated or clinical advice:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Allowing stimming to happen without social penalty</li>
      <li>Providing environments where stimming is accepted</li>
      <li>Recognizing increased stimming as a sign of high cognitive load (not misbehavior)</li>
      <li>Reducing the sources of overload (sensory input, concurrent demands, unpredictability) that trigger cascades in the first place</li>
    </ul>

    <p>When autistic people feel safe to stim freely, they often say they function better in every other domain. The way I'd put it: not because stimming provides regulation, but because they're not burning additional resources suppressing natural reactive outputs while simultaneously managing the overload that triggered them.</p>
