    <h2>DPM as a Proposed Structural Framework</h2>

    <p>Autism research has generated numerous theoretical frameworks, each capturing distinct aspects of autistic cognition and neurobiology. I do not see the Distributed Pathway Model as competing with these established theories. It is my attempt to offer a structural substrate that could show how they relate to one another. My reading is that each theory describes a different projection or level of analysis of the same underlying neural architecture. Here I try to connect these perspectives through circuit topology—branching density, inhibitory gating strength, and oscillatory dynamics—from which, if the DPM is right, the diverse phenomenological and computational features would follow.</p>

    <h3>Excitation/Inhibition (E/I) Imbalance</h3>

    <p>E/I imbalance theories propose that autism involves altered ratios of excitatory to inhibitory neurotransmission, typically favoring excitation through reduced GABAergic signaling, delayed inhibitory circuit maturation, or increased glutamatergic drive. Within DPM, I treat E/I balance as the control parameter that would govern distributed propagation. Reduced or delayed inhibition would lower the threshold for spillover from active pathways into reactive branches, increase the temporal persistence of activation before suppression, and allow concurrent engagement of partially overlapping circuits.</p>

    <p>DPM treats E/I imbalance not as a separate pathology but as the gating dynamic that enables distributed architecture to express itself. Inhibitory interneurons—particularly parvalbumin-positive (PV+) fast-spiking cells and somatostatin-positive (SST+) feedback regulators—normally constrain propagation both spatially (limiting spread across collaterals) and temporally (terminating activation rapidly). If this inhibitory constraint weakens, the preserved collateral branches could become functionally active routes rather than silent anatomical potentials. In DPM terms, E/I balance would set the active:reactive balance and the lifetime of reactive spread: stronger inhibition would favor active processing over reactive spillover, while weaker inhibition would let reactive pathways dominate.</p>

    <p>Findings often cited in support of E/I imbalance include reports of reduced GABA concentration measured via MRS, altered GAD67 and parvalbumin expression in post-mortem tissue, reduced gamma oscillation power (generated by PV+ circuits), and genetic variants affecting GABAergic signaling (e.g., GABRB3, GAD1). None of this was gathered to test the DPM. These findings are consistent with it, but E/I accounts predict most of the same things on their own, so I do not treat them as evidence for the DPM specifically. What the DPM adds is a way of positioning E/I balance as the regulator of how distributed topology is expressed.</p>

    <h3>Intense World Theory</h3>

    <p>Intense World Theory, developed from valproate rodent models, emphasizes hyper-reactivity, hyper-plasticity, and hyper-memory formation in autism. The theory proposes that autistic individuals experience the world with heightened intensity—sensory inputs produce stronger neural responses, memories are formed more readily and persist longer, and emotional reactions are amplified. DPM offers one possible circuit-level topology behind this intensity.</p>

    <p>Dense local branching and distributed propagation would amplify input: the same sensory stimulus could recruit more neurons through collateral pathways than streamlined hierarchical processing would. This would yield higher representational density—more neurons encoding finer distinctions—which I think is what intensity is from the inside. Hyper-plasticity could follow from sustained activation across distributed ensembles: when multiple partially overlapping populations encode the same event, synaptic strengthening may occur across these pathways at once, producing redundant encoding that resists forgetting. Hyper-reactivity would reflect weak inhibitory gating allowing excitatory spread to dominate.</p>

    <p>Intense World Theory describes the phenomenology—what autism feels like from the inside—while DPM is my attempt to describe the wiring that could generate that phenomenology. I see the two frameworks as complementary: my reading is that intensity may be the subjective correlate of distributed, high-resolution encoding.</p>

    <h3>Predictive Coding and HIPPEA (High, Inflexible Precision of Prediction Errors in Autism)</h3>

    <p>Predictive coding frameworks model perception and cognition as hierarchical Bayesian inference: top-down predictions are compared to bottom-up sensory signals, and prediction errors drive learning and updating. HIPPEA proposes that autism involves abnormally high precision-weighting of prediction errors—discrepancies between predictions and sensory data are treated as highly informative and difficult to downregulate, while priors (learned expectations) have reduced influence.</p>

    <p>DPM offers one account of how structure could yield this precision imbalance. Increased local branching would dedicate more neurons to encoding incoming sensory data, raising the representational weight of bottom-up signals. In predictive coding, precision describes how much a signal should be trusted; if more neurons encode sensory features, that could translate to higher precision for sensory input. At the same time, reduced long-range connectivity and weak hierarchical inhibition could limit the influence of top-down priors generated in higher cortical areas.</p>

    <p>In DPM, prediction errors would propagate through distributed pathways rather than being efficiently suppressed by top-down predictions. That broad propagation would raise the computational and subjective cost of uncertainty, which is how I would account for why unpredictability is so aversive for many autistic people. Preference for routine, resistance to change, and enhanced pattern recognition could all follow from a system that assigns high precision to sensory input and struggles to compress it via learned models. Predictive-coding accounts already predict most of this on their own, so the overlap is not evidence for the DPM; my suggestion is only that HIPPEA captures the computational consequence and DPM may describe a topology that could produce it.</p>

    <h3>Weak Central Coherence (WCC) and Enhanced Perceptual Functioning (EPF)</h3>

    <p>Weak Central Coherence theory proposes that autistic cognition favors local detail processing over global integration—difficulty seeing "the forest for the trees." Enhanced Perceptual Functioning complements this by emphasizing superior performance on tasks requiring local feature discrimination. These appear to describe opposite poles (deficit vs. strength), but I read them as two manifestations of the same architectural principle.</p>

    <p>Locally dense branching would create rich, detailed representations within circumscribed cortical modules, supporting exceptional perceptual discrimination (EPF). However, reduced long-range connectivity, fragmented oscillatory synchrony, and weak hierarchical gating would impair the integration of these local representations into coherent global percepts (WCC). On this reading, the same topology that enhances local resolution limits global binding.</p>

    <p>Neurophysiologically, this would correspond to local hyperconnectivity (short-range) coexisting with long-range hypoconnectivity. Many fMRI and DTI studies of autism report that pattern, which is consistent with, though not specific to, distributed architecture. Local gamma oscillations may be intact or enhanced in circumscribed regions, supporting local binding, while cross-region phase coherence may be reduced, impairing global integration. As I see it, WCC and EPF are not competing theories but complementary descriptions of distributed architecture's strengths and limitations.</p>

    <h3>Kozima's Granularity Theory</h3>

    <p>Granularity Theory proposes that autistic perception and cognition operate at finer granularity—smaller units of analysis, more micro-features concurrently represented, higher dimensional feature spaces. This maps directly onto DPM's concept of representational density. Increased branching would allow more neurons to sample the same stimulus, raising the sampling resolution—more dimensions, finer distinctions, higher granularity.</p>

    <p>By loose analogy with signal processing, denser sampling can capture finer features that coarser sampling would miss. This would enable detection of subtle patterns, fine sensory discriminations, and detailed memory encoding, but would also increase data volume, processing time, and energetic cost. Granularity Theory provides the information-theoretic perspective; DPM is my proposal for a circuit implementation.</p>

    <h3>Minicolumn Hypotheses and Synaptic Pruning Accounts</h3>

    <p>Some post-mortem histological work has reported structural alterations in autistic cortex: narrower minicolumns with reduced spacing, altered inhibitory surrounds, reduced dendritic branching complexity in some regions, and elevated synaptic spine density, which may reflect several mechanisms including reduced pruning and ongoing collateral formation. These findings were not gathered to test the DPM, but they are consistent with the topology it proposes and give it a possible histological anchor.</p>

    <p>Narrower minicolumns with reduced inhibitory surrounds could decrease lateral inhibition between adjacent processing units, facilitating crosstalk and distributed propagation. Elevated spine density may reflect both reduced synaptic pruning and ongoing collateral formation throughout life, preserving and generating additional collateral branches that would normally be eliminated or never formed. Altered GABAergic interneuron distribution (particularly PV+ basket cells surrounding minicolumns) fits DPM's proposal of weakened inhibitory gating.</p>

    <p>If the DPM is right, these structural findings would link molecular and genetic alterations (pruning regulators, interneuron migration factors) to circuit topology (branching, inhibition) to phenomenology (high resolution, overload, intensity).</p>

    <h3>Synthesis: DPM as a Proposed Structural Bridge</h3>

    <p>DPM is not a rival to these established theories; it is my attempt to organize them into a multi-level framework. Genetic and molecular findings (CHD8, SHANK3, PTEN, FMR1) bear on pruning, branching, and inhibitory development. My proposal is that these could produce circuit-level topology (distributed pathways, weak gating), that this topology could generate computational properties (precision-weighting, granularity, local hyperconnectivity), and that these computational properties might show up as phenomenology (intensity, sensory acuity, detail focus, overload).</p>

    <p>On this reading, each existing theory captures one level or projection of this cascade. E/I imbalance describes the gating mechanism. Intense World describes the phenomenology. Predictive coding describes the computational consequence. WCC/EPF describe the integration trade-offs. Minicolumn accounts offer a histological anchor. DPM is my attempt at a structural bridge: a way of saying how differences in pruning, ongoing synaptogenesis, inhibition, and channel dynamics could manifest as distributed pathway behavior, and how the phenomenology might follow. For what would make me more or less confident in that, see the paper's section <a href="../hrh-paper#8-what-would-change-my-mind-and-what-i-dont-know-how-to-test">What would change my mind, and what I don't know how to test</a>.</p>

    <h2>How DPM Connects Existing Autism Theories</h2>

    <p>The Distributed Pathway Model is not intended to replace existing autism theories. It is my attempt at a structural framework that could show how diverse theoretical accounts relate to one another. As I read them, each established theory captures a different level or aspect of autistic cognition; DPM proposes a circuit-level topology that might connect them.</p>

    <h3>Excitation/Inhibition (E/I) Imbalance: The Gating Mechanism</h3>

    <p><strong>E/I Imbalance theory</strong> proposes that autism involves altered balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission, typically favoring excitation through reduced GABAergic signaling or increased glutamatergic drive. This theory offers a reason why neural signals might spread more broadly and persist longer in autism.</p>

    <p>Within DPM, I treat E/I balance as the control parameter that would govern distributed propagation. Inhibitory neurons—the brain's brakes—normally constrain how far signals spread (spatially) and how long they last (temporally). If inhibition is reduced or delayed, the preserved collateral branches proposed by DPM could become functionally active pathways rather than dormant anatomical structures. In DPM terms, E/I balance would set the active:reactive balance: stronger inhibition would favor goal-directed processing with limited spillover, while weaker inhibition would allow reactive pathways to dominate.</p>

    <p>In this framing, E/I imbalance is not a separate dysfunction from distributed architecture—it's the gating dynamic that would let distributed topology express itself functionally. The branches would exist anatomically due to both reduced pruning and ongoing collateral formation; E/I balance would determine whether signals actually propagate through them. Most findings cited for E/I imbalance are consistent with the DPM but not specific to it, since E/I accounts predict most of the same things on their own.</p>

    <h3>Intense World Theory: The Phenomenology of Distributed Processing</h3>

    <p><strong>Intense World Theory</strong> emphasizes that autistic individuals experience heightened intensity—stronger sensory responses, deeper emotional reactions, more persistent memories. This theory emerged from animal models showing hyper-reactivity and hyper-plasticity.</p>

    <p>DPM offers one possible structural account of this intensity. Dense local branching means the same sensory input may recruit more neurons through distributed pathways than in typical processing. That would create higher representational density: more neurons encoding finer distinctions, which I think is experienced as intensity. Hyper-plasticity could follow: when multiple partially overlapping neural populations encode the same event, synaptic strengthening may occur across these pathways at once, creating redundant, persistent memories.</p>

    <p>Intense World describes what autism feels like; DPM is my attempt to describe the neural wiring that could produce that feeling. I see the two frameworks as complementary perspectives.</p>

    <h3>Predictive Coding and HIPPEA: Computational Consequences</h3>

    <p><strong>Predictive coding theories</strong> model cognition as Bayesian inference: the brain generates predictions about incoming information and updates these predictions when they don't match reality. <strong>HIPPEA (High, Inflexible Precision of Prediction Errors in Autism)</strong> proposes that autistic cognition weighs sensory input more heavily than learned expectations, making prediction errors particularly salient and difficult to downregulate.</p>

    <p>DPM offers one account of how structure could yield this precision imbalance. More local branches would dedicate more neurons to representing bottom-up sensory signals, increasing the weight of "what's actually happening" relative to "what we expect to happen." In predictive coding terms, this would mean higher precision-weighting for sensory data and lower influence for top-down priors.</p>

    <p>In DPM, when prediction errors occur—when reality doesn't match expectations—they would propagate through distributed pathways rather than being efficiently suppressed by top-down predictions. That would amplify both the computational cost (more pathways to update) and the subjective cost (more salient mismatch signal). This is how I would account for preference for routine (minimizing prediction errors), resistance to change (high cost of updating), and exceptional pattern detection (high-resolution encoding of actual data)—though predictive-coding accounts predict most of the same things without the DPM.</p>

    <p>HIPPEA describes the computational consequence; DPM proposes a topology that could produce it.</p>

    <h3>Weak Central Coherence and Enhanced Perceptual Functioning: Two Sides of the Same Architecture</h3>

    <p><strong>Weak Central Coherence (WCC)</strong> proposes that autistic cognition favors processing local details over global integration—"missing the forest for the trees." <strong>Enhanced Perceptual Functioning (EPF)</strong> emphasizes superior performance on tasks requiring fine perceptual discrimination. These theories appear to describe opposites—deficit versus strength—but my reading is that they're two faces of the same architectural coin.</p>

    <p>Locally dense branching would create rich, detailed representations within local brain regions, supporting exceptional discrimination of fine features (EPF). However, reduced long-range connectivity and fragmented oscillatory synchrony would make it harder to integrate these detailed local representations into coherent global percepts (WCC). On this view, the same topology that enhances local resolution impairs global binding.</p>

    <p>Many fMRI and DTI studies report local hyperconnectivity (short-range) coexisting with long-range hypoconnectivity in autism. That is consistent with, though not specific to, this picture. Local neural populations may synchronize well within themselves, enabling detailed local processing, while synchronization across distant regions may be fragmented, limiting global integration.</p>

    <p>As I see it, WCC and EPF aren't competing theories—they're complementary descriptions of distributed architecture's computational trade-offs.</p>

    <h3>Granularity Theory: Information-Theoretic Perspective</h3>

    <p><strong>Granularity Theory</strong> proposes that autistic perception and cognition operate at finer granularity—processing smaller units of information, representing more micro-features simultaneously, operating in higher-dimensional feature spaces.</p>

    <p>This maps directly onto DPM's representational density. More neural branches sampling the same stimulus would mean higher sampling resolution—like increasing pixel count in a digital image. This would enable detection of finer distinctions and subtle patterns but also increase processing time, memory load, and energetic cost.</p>

    <p>Granularity Theory provides the information-theoretic description; DPM is my proposal for a circuit implementation that could produce that granularity.</p>

    <h3>Minicolumn and Pruning Hypotheses: An Anatomical Anchor</h3>

    <p><strong>Minicolumn hypotheses</strong> describe structural brain differences reported in some post-mortem autism studies: narrower cortical minicolumns, altered inhibitory surrounds, and elevated synaptic spine density, which may reflect several mechanisms including reduced pruning and ongoing collateral formation. None of this was gathered to test the DPM; the findings are consistent with the topology it proposes, which is not the same as being evidence for it.</p>

    <p>Narrower minicolumns with reduced inhibitory surrounds could facilitate crosstalk between adjacent processing modules, promoting distributed propagation. Elevated spine density may reflect both reduced synaptic pruning and continued collateral formation throughout life—the collateral branches that DPM proposes are being preserved and continuously generated beyond early developmental periods. Altered distribution of inhibitory interneurons fits DPM's proposal of weakened inhibitory gating.</p>

    <p>If the DPM is right, these structural findings would give it an anatomical anchor, linking molecular causes (genes affecting pruning and inhibition) to circuit topology (branching patterns) to cognitive outcomes (high resolution, intensity, overload).</p>

    <h3>DPM as Organizing Framework</h3>

    <p>I offer DPM as a possible structural bridge connecting different levels of analysis:</p>

    <ul>
      <li><strong>Molecular level:</strong> Genes affecting pruning, ongoing synapse formation and stabilization, inhibitory development</li>
      <li><strong>Circuit level:</strong> Distributed branching, weak inhibitory gating, altered oscillations</li>
      <li><strong>Computational level:</strong> Precision-weighting, granularity, local-global trade-offs</li>
      <li><strong>Phenomenological level:</strong> Intensity, sensory acuity, detail focus, overload, predictability preference</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Each existing theory captures one level or aspect of this cascade. E/I imbalance describes the gating mechanism. Intense World describes the phenomenology. Predictive coding describes computational consequences. WCC/EPF describe integration trade-offs. Minicolumn accounts offer an anatomical anchor.</p>

    <p>DPM isn't a rival to these theories. It is my attempt to organize them into a multi-level framework: a way of saying how molecular differences could produce circuit changes, which could generate computational properties that show up as lived experience. For what would make me more or less confident in that, see <a href="../hrh-paper#8-what-would-change-my-mind-and-what-i-dont-know-how-to-test">What would change my mind, and what I don't know how to test</a> in the paper.</p>

    <h2>How Different Autism Theories Fit Together</h2>

    <p>Over the years, researchers have developed many different theories to explain autism. You might have heard of some of them: Intense World Theory, Excitation/Inhibition Imbalance, Predictive Coding, Weak Central Coherence. These theories can seem contradictory or confusing—like different people describing different conditions.</p>

    <p>The Distributed Pathway Model doesn't replace these theories. Instead, it's my attempt to show how they might fit together—like puzzle pieces from the same picture. My sense is that each theory is describing a different aspect of the same underlying brain architecture.</p>

    <h3>E/I Imbalance: The Volume Control</h3>

    <p><strong>What the theory says:</strong> Excitation/Inhibition (E/I) Imbalance theory proposes that autistic brains have an imbalance between signals that activate neurons (excitation) and signals that quiet them down (inhibition). Typically, there's too much excitation or too little inhibition, like a car with a weak brake system.</p>

    <p><strong>How DPM connects it:</strong> Remember how DPM describes the brain's "brake system"—inhibitory neurons that stop signals from spreading too far? E/I imbalance is the theory that tries to explain <em>why</em> those brakes might be weaker in autism.</p>

    <p>Think of it this way:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>DPM says:</strong> "Your brain has many extra pathways preserved from development" (the road network has more roads)</li>
      <li><strong>E/I Imbalance says:</strong> "Your traffic lights and stop signs don't work as well" (the control system is weaker)</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Put together, they suggest a fuller picture: more pathways <em>and</em> weaker control over them. In this model, E/I balance acts like a volume knob for how much distributed processing happens—weak inhibition turns the volume up, strong inhibition turns it down.</p>

    <h3>Intense World Theory: How It Feels</h3>

    <p><strong>What the theory says:</strong> Intense World Theory proposes that autistic people experience everything more intensely—sensory input feels stronger, memories are more vivid and persistent, emotional reactions are more powerful. It's not that you're being "oversensitive"—the world genuinely registers more intensely in your brain.</p>

    <p><strong>How DPM connects it:</strong> DPM offers one possible reason <em>why</em> everything might feel more intense. If distributed pathways activate more neurons for the same input, I would expect the resulting experience to be more vivid, detailed, and persistent.</p>

    <p>Imagine two people watching the same sunset:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Neurotypical brain (compressed processing):</strong> Registers "sunset, orange, pretty" with moderate emotional response</li>
      <li><strong>Autistic brain (distributed processing):</strong> Simultaneously registers the precise gradient of colors, the way light refracts through clouds, the temperature shift, the sound of evening birds, the emotional associations with previous sunsets, the texture of the air—all at once, all intensely</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Intense World Theory describes <em>what it feels like</em>. DPM tries to describe <em>the neural wiring that could make it feel that way</em>. I think of them as looking at the same thing from different angles—experience versus mechanism.</p>

    <h3>Predictive Coding: Why Surprises Hit Harder</h3>

    <p><strong>What the theory says:</strong> Predictive coding theories propose that your brain constantly predicts what will happen next, then compares predictions to reality. In autism, the theory suggests that sensory reality (what's actually happening) is weighted much more heavily than predictions (what you expect to happen). This makes unexpected changes particularly jarring.</p>

    <p><strong>How DPM connects it:</strong> DPM offers one possible reason <em>why</em> your brain might weight sensory input so heavily. More local branches would mean more neurons dedicated to encoding what's actually there, making "reality" louder than "expectations" in the brain's processing.</p>

    <p>Think of it like weather forecasting:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Neurotypical brain:</strong> Uses historical averages heavily (it's usually sunny in July, so probably sunny today) and trusts predictions even when current data differs slightly</li>
      <li><strong>Autistic brain:</strong> Weights current sensor data heavily (the barometer is falling, humidity is rising, clouds are forming) and treats any mismatch with predictions as highly significant</li>
    </ul>

    <p>When predictions turn out wrong, distributed pathways would mean the "error signal" spreads through many circuits, making the mismatch feel very significant. That could help account for:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Why unexpected changes are so distressing (large error signal spreading widely)</li>
      <li>Why routines are comforting (minimizing prediction errors)</li>
      <li>Why you notice patterns others miss (high-resolution encoding of actual data)</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Predictive coding theories already explain much of this on their own. What DPM adds is a guess about the wiring that could produce it.</p>

    <h3>Weak Central Coherence vs. Enhanced Perceptual Functioning: Two Sides of One Coin</h3>

    <p><strong>What the theories say:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Weak Central Coherence (WCC):</strong> Autistic people tend to focus on details rather than the big picture—difficulty "seeing the forest for the trees"</li>
      <li><strong>Enhanced Perceptual Functioning (EPF):</strong> Autistic people excel at noticing fine details and discriminating subtle differences</li>
    </ul>

    <p>These sound like opposites—one a "deficit," one a "strength"—but I think they may be describing the same thing from different perspectives.</p>

    <p><strong>How DPM connects them:</strong> Imagine looking at a painting:</p>

    <p><strong>Neurotypical brain (compressed processing):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Quickly captures overall composition (good global integration)</li>
      <li>Sees "a portrait of a woman" (central coherence)</li>
      <li>May miss individual brushstrokes (lower local resolution)</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Autistic brain (distributed processing):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Notices every brushstroke, color variation, texture detail (enhanced local processing = EPF)</li>
      <li>May take longer to integrate these details into "overall portrait of a woman" (weak global integration = WCC)</li>
    </ul>

    <p>The same distributed architecture that could create exceptional detail perception (EPF) would make it harder to quickly integrate those details into a global summary (WCC). The way I see it, these aren't two separate traits—they're a trade-off of high-resolution processing.</p>

    <p>In DPM terms: dense local branching would enhance detail encoding, while weaker long-range connections would make global integration harder. My reading is that WCC and EPF are both describing this same pattern.</p>

    <h3>Granularity Theory: The Resolution Setting</h3>

    <p><strong>What the theory says:</strong> Granularity Theory proposes that autistic perception and cognition operate at finer granularity—smaller units of analysis, more micro-features, higher detail resolution.</p>

    <p><strong>How DPM connects it:</strong> This maps directly onto DPM's "representational density." Think of it like image resolution:</p>

    <ul>
      <li><strong>Standard resolution (neurotypical):</strong> an ordinary photo—captures the scene clearly, good for most purposes</li>
      <li><strong>High resolution (autistic):</strong> a much higher-resolution photo—captures detail that would be invisible in the ordinary one</li>
    </ul>

    <p>More neural branches sampling the same stimulus would mean higher sampling resolution, which would mean finer granularity. As I see it, it's the same concept in different terminology.</p>

    <p>Granularity Theory describes it from an information-processing perspective. DPM is my guess at the neural implementation.</p>

    <h3>Minicolumn and Pruning Theories: The Physical Side</h3>

    <p><strong>What the theories say:</strong> Some studies of autistic brain tissue have reported physical differences:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Narrower cortical minicolumns (organizational units in the cortex)</li>
      <li>Altered inhibitory neuron patterns around these columns</li>
      <li>More synaptic connections (higher spine density)</li>
      <li>Signs that pruning during development may have been less aggressive</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>How DPM connects them:</strong> These findings weren't gathered to test DPM, but they fit with what it proposes:</p>

    <ul>
      <li><strong>Higher spine density</strong> could be the preserved and continuously generated extra connections that DPM describes</li>
      <li><strong>Narrower minicolumns with altered inhibition</strong> could be the weaker "brake system" between processing units</li>
      <li><strong>Reduced pruning plus ongoing collateral formation</strong> could be the mechanisms that both preserve and keep generating the extra pathways throughout life</li>
    </ul>

    <p>If DPM is right, these would be the anatomical structures—the physical wiring—behind distributed architecture. Fitting with a model isn't the same as proving it, though.</p>

    <p>As I see it, these aren't competing explanations of different conditions. They may be complementary perspectives on the same underlying neural architecture—your brain's particular wiring pattern and how it could shape autistic experience.</p>

    <h3>Why This Matters</h3>

    <p>Thinking about how these theories might connect can help in a few ways:</p>

    <p><strong>Makes sense of seemingly contradictory information:</strong> "Enhanced perception" and "weak coherence" aren't contradictory—they're two sides of the same architectural coin.</p>

    <p><strong>Making sense of your specific experience:</strong> You might relate strongly to some aspects (intense sensory experiences) and less to others (you might have good global integration in some domains). The framework offers one possible reason—distributed architecture may express differently across brain regions.</p>

    <p><strong>Thinking about support:</strong> Different theories suggest different approaches, and seeing how they might connect could help in thinking about which ones address the same underlying pattern from different angles. None of this has been evaluated, and it isn't clinical advice.</p>

    <p><strong>Taking lived experience seriously:</strong> If the model is right, the intensity isn't imagined—it would reflect how your brain is wired. The detail focus isn't a "deficit"—it would be a trade-off. The framework is my attempt to show that autistic experience can be read as a coherent pattern, not random dysfunction.</p>

    <p>My sense is that these theories are describing the same brain from different perspectives. DPM is my attempt to show how they might fit together.</p>
