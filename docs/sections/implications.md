    <h2>Reframing Intervention: From Normalization to Optimization</h2>

    <p>Everything on this page is what I would try if the model were right; none of it has been evaluated, and none of it is clinical advice.</p>

    <p>If the Distributed Pathway Model is right, it would reframe how intervention is approached. Rather than treating autism as a collection of deficits requiring remediation to approximate neurotypical function, the DPM treats autistic neurocognitive architecture as an alternative processing configuration with its own properties, strengths, and energetic constraints. On that view, I would want support to work within this architecture rather than try to restructure it toward typical topology. That would touch clinical practice, educational design, workplace accommodation, therapy, and research.</p>

    <h3>Clinical Reconceptualization: Collapse States as Metabolic Phenomena</h3>

    <p>My reading is that meltdowns and shutdowns are not behavioral anomalies, emotional dysregulation failures, or willful non-compliance, but states that may emerge when distributed processing exceeds metabolic capacity. If that is right, I would want assessment to focus on individual energy budgets, collapse precursors (increased stimming, reduced speech, cognitive slowdown), and load factors (concurrent demands, sensory saturation, predictive interference) rather than treating observable behaviors as the primary targets.</p>

    <p>The priorities I would try are: reducing concurrent cognitive and sensory load before collapse sets in; treating recovery as something that may need real restoration over hours to days rather than behavioral redirection; recognizing that trying to function during recovery could restart the depletion; and accepting that external load assessments ("they're not doing that much") may miss internal processing demands. The paper offers the idea that brief breaks interrupt the depletion cycle early as an observation that fits, not as clinical guidance.</p>

    <h3>Inhibitory Precision and Temporal Gating</h3>

    <p>If the model is right, the relevant difference lies in inhibitory timing and strength rather than in connectivity density itself, and reducing connectivity would remove the basis for high-resolution processing. Research on inhibitory interneurons (for example PV+ and SST+ populations) and on the balance between excitation and inhibition is the area I would watch. I am not recommending any drug, supplement, or neuromodulation technique; none has been evaluated against this model, and E/I imbalance accounts point in much the same direction without needing the DPM.</p>

    <p>My one caution is conceptual: if distributed processing is what supports some strengths, then anything that simply increased inhibition across the board could, on this model, cost those strengths as well.</p>

    <h3>Stimming as Reactive Motor Cascade and Suppression Costs</h3>

    <p>Stimming arises as a reactive motor cascade when distributed activation exceeds inhibitory containment and spills into motor circuits. This document does not model stimming as producing regulatory or stabilization effects; rather, stimming emerges when distributed activity finds its path of least resistance—motor pathways with weakly gated connections (see <a href="stimming-reactive-loops">Stimming & Reactive Loops</a>).</p>

    <p>What I would expect is that forcibly suppressing reactive motor cascades carries a cost. Top-down suppression would recruit additional control networks (prefrontal cortex) and inhibitory drive (amygdala-mediated suppression under social threat), which could raise local energy demand and destabilize processing. If cascades are suppressed, activation that would have become movement may stay distributed across internal circuits, adding to inhibitory and energetic load. For that reason I would favor accommodating stimming over suppressing it, since suppression seems costly without addressing the activation that triggered the cascade.</p>

    <h3>Predictability and Prediction Error Minimization</h3>

    <p>If distributed architecture assigns high precision to sensory input and propagates prediction errors broadly, unpredictability would be metabolically expensive. Structured routines, predictable transitions, and transparent contingencies would then reduce prediction-error load. On this reading, preference for routine looks less like rigidity and more like reasonable energy management in a system where uncertainty costs more. (Predictive-coding accounts such as HIPPEA suggest much the same thing without the DPM.)</p>

    <p>In clinical and educational settings, I would try advance notification of changes, consistent sequencing, and explicit communication of expectations. When changes are necessary, detailed information about what will change, when, and what the new pattern will be may let the system update its predictions rather than operate under sustained uncertainty.</p>

    <h3>Deep Focus and Special Interests as Optimal Operating States</h3>

    <p>When distributed pathways converge on a domain with well-established internal models and high intrinsic interest, I would expect prediction error to drop, processing to become self-reinforcing, and energy use to become more efficient. This is how I would account for deep focus and special-interest engagement. Rather than viewing these as restrictive or maladaptive, I read them as states in which the architecture's strengths come through with fewer of its costs.</p>

    <p>If that is right, education and work would do well to permit sustained engagement with areas of strength rather than forcing constant domain-switching. Curricula organized around deep projects, with autonomy and room for prolonged focus, would play to the system's strengths rather than fight its inertia. Workplaces with flexible schedules, minimal task-switching, and room for specialized expertise may let autistic people work in more energy-efficient states.</p>

    <h3>Task Decomposition and External Scaffolding</h3>

    <p>My reading is that distributed processing tends to decompose tasks into finer micro-steps, because more pathways encode more contingencies and decision points. Providing explicit task decomposition externally—breaking projects into numbered steps, providing checklists, making implicit contingencies explicit—could reduce the load of holding all possible branches in working memory at once.</p>

    <p>I do not see this as "hand-holding" or lowered expectations; it is external scaffolding that would match the internal processing architecture. If the model is right, neurotypical people do similar decomposition implicitly through hierarchical compression, and autistic people may benefit from receiving it explicitly.</p>

    <h3>Sensory Environment Design</h3>

    <p>If distributed architecture over-samples sensory input—more neurons encoding more dimensions of each stimulus, with weaker inhibition allowing several streams to be processed at once—then environments with unpredictable multimodal noise (overlapping conversations, fluorescent flicker, variable ambient sound, thermal fluctuations) would impose much greater processing demands than quiet, predictable ones.</p>

    <p>The sensory design I would try includes: user-controllable lighting (dimmable, non-flickering LED), acoustic damping (reducing reverberation and ambient noise), thermal stability, predictable transition cues (visual schedules, advance warnings), and sensory refuges (quiet spaces for recovery). I think of these less as "special accommodations" for disability and more as ergonomics for a different processing architecture, comparable to providing appropriate desk height.</p>

    <h3>Therapeutic Framing: Structural Rather Than Volitional</h3>

    <p>If the model is right, cognitive-behavioral and psychotherapeutic approaches would do better to frame challenges as emerging from neural architecture rather than from volitional choices or psychological deficits. On this model, difficulty switching tasks looks less like poor executive function requiring more effort and more like the structural cost of deactivating distributed pathways; sensory overload looks less like hypersensitivity requiring desensitization and more like a consequence of high-resolution encoding.</p>

    <p>I would hope this reframing reduces misplaced self-blame and supports realistic self-understanding. Therapeutic goals would shift from "improving executive function" toward "managing energy budgets," and from "increasing sensory tolerance" toward "identifying load limits and planning recovery." Therapies that emphasize interoceptive awareness, pacing, and predictable sequencing may support self-regulation more effectively than purely behavioral approaches—learning to recognize early signs of approaching collapse could allow load to be reduced before a crisis.</p>

    <h3>Developmental Windows and Plasticity</h3>

    <p>Early neurodevelopment includes periods when inhibitory circuit maturation and synaptic pruning are especially plastic. If the model is right, support during those periods might influence the balance between active and reactive pathways more than later support would. This is speculative; I have no evidence for it, and I would not want anyone to act on it as guidance.</p>

    <p>If this direction were ever explored, I would want it to preserve representational richness rather than reduce branching, and to favor gentle, low-risk supports—predictable structure and sensory-motor environments that fit the child—over anything invasive. None of this has been evaluated.</p>

    <h3>Future Research Directions</h3>

    <p>The paper does not offer a list of formal predictions. Instead it sets out what would make me more or less confident in the model, and where I do not know how to test it at all (see <a href="../hrh-paper#8-what-would-change-my-mind-and-what-i-dont-know-how-to-test">What would change my mind, and what I don't know how to test</a>). Much of what the model says overlaps with E/I imbalance and predictive-coding accounts, so a finding that fits the DPM will often fit those as well.</p>

    <p>Work that combines structural and functional connectivity, oscillatory dynamics, and metabolic measures might eventually say something about the questions here, but some of what the model asserts—energy competition at the scale of cortical columns, for example—cannot be measured with current methods. Behavioral work on the cost of unpredictability, the value of task decomposition, and deep focus would be closer to hand.</p>

    <p>Longitudinal work across development and life contexts, and careful integration of lived experience with objective measures, would help show whether the framework holds up at all—and, if it does not, where it breaks.</p>

    <h3>Paradigm Shift: Accommodation Rather Than Remediation</h3>

    <p>The implication I care most about is a shift from deficit remediation toward accommodation. As I see it, distributed processing is not broken hierarchical processing but an alternative configuration with different strengths, costs, and conditions under which it works well. On that view, the goal would not be to make autistic cognition approximate neurotypical patterns, but to create environments, supports, and strategies that let it function well.</p>

    <p>This reframing has ethical implications. Viewed through the DPM, autism looks less like a disorder and more like neurocognitive variance—different, not deficient. Accommodations become ergonomics rather than special treatment, and self-advocacy becomes a kind of self-knowledge about one's own architecture. The aim is not normalization but letting autistic people work within their strengths while managing their constraints, as anyone does within their own neurobiology. These implications may also extend beyond autism, to how we think about attention, creativity, and resilience in all brains.</p>

    <h2>Practical Implications for Support and Intervention</h2>

    <p>If autism reflects a distributed neural architecture rather than a collection of deficits, that would change how support, intervention, and accommodation are approached. The goal would shift from trying to normalize atypical processing to helping a different but valid cognitive configuration work well. Everything below is what I would try if the model were right; none of it has been evaluated, and it is not clinical advice.</p>

    <h3>Clinical Reconceptualization</h3>

    <p>Meltdowns, shutdowns, and stimming are common outcomes within a distributed system, and I do not think they are best treated as behavioral problems requiring suppression. My reading is that meltdowns may reflect excitatory cascade exceeding inhibitory capacity, shutdowns may reflect metabolic collapse when energy demand exceeds supply, and stimming may reflect reactive motor cascade—involuntary output when distributed activation spills into motor circuits.</p>

    <p>I would expect support aimed at energy recovery and inhibitory precision (through rest cycles, rhythmic activities, or structured predictability) to help more than approaches that suppress expression. That would mean identifying individual energy budgets, recognizing early warning signs of approaching collapse (increased stimming, reduced speech, cognitive slowdown), and reducing load before it builds. Recovery may need real restoration over hours to days rather than behavioral redirection, and trying to function during recovery could restart the depletion.</p>

    <p>I would want assessment to consider internal processing demands, not only external task difficulty. Two people doing the same task may carry very different internal loads depending on how their architecture is configured.</p>

    <h3>Sensory Environment Design</h3>

    <p>If distributed architecture samples sensory input with greater breadth and persistence than typical processing, environments with unpredictable multimodal stimulation (overlapping conversations, fluorescent flicker, variable ambient sound, thermal fluctuations) would create much greater processing demands. Spaces that minimize unpredictable noise and offer controllable stimuli may help.</p>

    <p>The sensory design I would try provides:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Controllable lighting:</strong> Dimmable, non-flickering LED rather than fluorescent; user control over brightness</li>
      <li><strong>Acoustic damping:</strong> Sound-absorbing materials reducing reverberation; white noise masking unpredictable sounds</li>
      <li><strong>Thermal stability:</strong> Consistent temperature and airflow</li>
      <li><strong>Predictable transitions:</strong> Visual schedules, advance warnings, explicit communication of changes</li>
      <li><strong>Sensory refuges:</strong> Quiet spaces for recovery when approaching overload</li>
    </ul>

    <p>I think of these accommodations as ergonomics for distributed processing rather than special treatment for disability—comparable to providing appropriate desk height or lighting for any worker.</p>

    <h3>Educational Implications</h3>

    <p>My reading is that deep focus on special interests is a state in which distributed pathways converge on well-established internal models, which I would expect to reduce prediction error and make processing more energy-efficient. Rather than viewing this as restrictive or obsessive, I would want education to treat it as a strength to build on.</p>

    <p>Educational approaches I would try include:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Project-based learning:</strong> Sustained engagement with topics of interest rather than frequent subject switching</li>
      <li><strong>Explicit task decomposition:</strong> Breaking complex projects into numbered steps rather than expecting implicit planning</li>
      <li><strong>Autonomy and flexibility:</strong> Allowing individual pacing and approach rather than rigid timelines</li>
      <li><strong>Reduced concurrent demands:</strong> Minimizing multitasking requirements</li>
      <li><strong>Predictable structure:</strong> Consistent schedules, advance notification of changes</li>
    </ul>

    <p>I do not see task decomposition as "hand-holding"—it is external scaffolding that would match the internal processing architecture. If the model is right, neurotypical students do similar decomposition implicitly, and autistic students may benefit from receiving it explicitly.</p>

    <h3>Workplace Accommodation</h3>

    <p>Traditional workplace structures often conflict with distributed processing: frequent meetings requiring rapid context-switching, open floor plans with unpredictable sensory input, implicit expectations and social navigation, concurrent project management.</p>

    <p>Workplace accommodations I would try include:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Minimal task-switching:</strong> Allowing sustained focus on specialized work rather than constant context changes</li>
      <li><strong>Written communication:</strong> Email/chat preferred over impromptu verbal requests, providing processing time</li>
      <li><strong>Quiet workspaces:</strong> Private offices or noise-canceling headphones; control over sensory environment</li>
      <li><strong>Explicit expectations:</strong> Clear deliverables and deadlines rather than implicit social understanding</li>
      <li><strong>Flexible schedules:</strong> Accommodating energy patterns and allowing recovery time</li>
      <li><strong>Specialized roles:</strong> Leveraging exceptional pattern recognition, attention to detail, systematic thinking</li>
    </ul>

    <p>My expectation is that these accommodations would support productivity rather than reduce it, by letting distributed architecture work in its areas of strength.</p>

    <h3>Therapeutic Approaches</h3>

    <p>If the model is right, cognitive-behavioral and psychotherapeutic approaches would do better to frame challenges as structural features of neural architecture rather than volitional choices or psychological deficits. On this model, difficulty switching tasks looks less like a lack of willpower and more like the cost of deactivating distributed pathways, and sensory overload looks less like hypersensitivity and more like a consequence of high-resolution encoding. Therapies that emphasize interoceptive awareness, pacing, and predictable sequencing may support self-regulation more effectively than purely behavioral approaches.</p>

    <p>Therapeutic goals I would expect to help include:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Energy budget management:</strong> Learning to recognize capacity limits and manage demands accordingly</li>
      <li><strong>Interoceptive awareness:</strong> Identifying early signs of approaching collapse</li>
      <li><strong>Pacing strategies:</strong> Building in recovery periods before depletion</li>
      <li><strong>Realistic self-understanding:</strong> Recognizing architectural strengths and constraints without self-blame</li>
      <li><strong>Environmental modification:</strong> Identifying and creating supportive conditions</li>
    </ul>

    <p>I would hope this reframing reduces misplaced guilt and supports practical strategies grounded in an understanding of how one's own processing works.</p>

    <h3>Stimming as Reactive Cascade and Suppression Costs</h3>

    <p>Stimming emerges as a reactive motor cascade when distributed activation exceeds inhibitory containment and spills into motor circuits. Some individuals report subjective experiences of calm or focus associated with stimming; however, these phenomenological reports fall outside the scope of mechanistic modeling. This framework does not model stimming as producing stabilization—only as reactive output from distributed propagation (see <a href="stimming-reactive-loops">Stimming & Reactive Loops</a>).</p>

    <p>I would expect suppressing stimming to recruit additional control networks and inhibitory drive, raising energetic load. Top-down suppression (PFC-driven) and amygdala-mediated suppression under social threat would both add metabolic demand while leaving the underlying activation unaddressed, which could compound existing overload. For that reason I would favor accommodating stimming in therapeutic and educational settings rather than suppressing it.</p>

    <h3>Predictability as Energy Management</h3>

    <p>If distributed architecture assigns high precision to sensory input and propagates prediction errors broadly, unpredictability would be metabolically expensive. On this reading, preference for routine and resistance to change look less like rigidity and more like reasonable energy management. (Predictive-coding accounts suggest much the same without the DPM.)</p>

    <p>Supportive environments provide:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Advance notification of changes with as much detail as possible</li>
      <li>Consistent sequencing and structure</li>
      <li>Explicit communication of expectations and contingencies</li>
      <li>Transparent reasoning when changes are necessary</li>
    </ul>

    <p>This may allow predictions to be updated rather than leaving the system operating under sustained uncertainty, which I would expect to lower baseline energy demands.</p>

    <p>If the model is right, the relevant difference lies in inhibitory timing and strength rather than connectivity density, so research into inhibitory interneurons and excitation/inhibition balance is the area I would watch.     <p>One conceptual caution: if distributed processing supports some strengths, then anything that simply increased inhibition across the board could, on this model, cost those strengths as well.</p>

    <h3>Research Priorities</h3>

    <p>The paper does not list formal predictions. It describes what would make me more or less confident in the model and where I do not know how to test it (see <a href="../hrh-paper#8-what-would-change-my-mind-and-what-i-dont-know-how-to-test">What would change my mind, and what I don't know how to test</a>). Questions I would like to see explored include:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Whether the balance between active and reactive pathways can be characterized at all with current imaging methods</li>
      <li>How individual energy budgets and collapse patterns differ from person to person</li>
      <li>Whether structured predictability actually reduces load, and whether that helps in practice</li>
      <li>Whether task decomposition and external scaffolding help, and for whom</li>
      <li>How these patterns change across development</li>

    <h3>Paradigm Shift: Optimization, Not Normalization</h3>

    <p>The implication I care most about is a shift from viewing autism as a disorder requiring normalization toward seeing it as neurocognitive variance that calls for accommodation.     <p>On that view, the goal would be environments, supports, and strategies that let distributed architecture work well—operating within its strengths while managing its constraints. Accommodation becomes ergonomics rather than special treatment, and support shifts from remediation toward helping autistic people thrive as they are. These ideas may also extend beyond autism, to attention, creativity, and resilience in all brains.</p>

    <h2>What This Means for Real Life</h2>

    <p>If autism really is a different way of being wired—what I call distributed neural architecture—that would change a lot about how support and accommodation work. The goal wouldn't be to make autistic people process information "normally." It would be to create environments and strategies that let distributed processing work well.</p>

    <p><em>A quick note first:</em> the ideas on this page are what I would try if the model is right. None of them has been tested, and none of this is medical advice.</p>

    <h3>The Big Shift: From "Fix" to "Optimize"</h3>

    <p>Traditional approaches to autism often focus on trying to make autistic people act more neurotypical—reduce stimming, improve eye contact, increase social interaction, practice flexibility. This is like trying to make a high-resolution camera work like a standard camera by degrading the image quality.</p>

    <p>The approach I'd suggest is different: <strong>work with the architecture, not against it.</strong></p>

    <p>Think of it like ergonomics. We don't tell left-handed people to just try harder to be right-handed. We design left-handed scissors and desks. Similarly, we shouldn't tell autistic people to just try harder to process like neurotypicals. We should design environments and supports that work with distributed processing.</p>

    <h3>What I Think Meltdowns and Shutdowns Are (And What Might Help)</h3>

    <p><strong>What they're NOT:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Tantrums or attention-seeking behavior</li>
      <li>Lack of emotional control that could be fixed with better coping skills</li>
      <li>Manipulation or avoidance</li>
      <li>Something you can "push through" with willpower</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>What I think they are:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Energy collapse—my best guess is that parts of the brain are running low on fuel faster than it can be delivered</li>
      <li>Biological limits being reached, not psychological choices</li>
      <li>Possibly a kind of protective shutdown when the system is overloaded</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>What might help:</strong></p>

    <p><em>Prevention (what I'd put first):</em></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Learn your early warning signs (increased stimming, irritability, slower thinking, reduced speech)</li>
      <li>Reduce demands BEFORE you hit the wall, not after</li>
      <li>Build in regular breaks and recovery time</li>
      <li>Accept that your energy budget is real and has limits</li>
    </ul>

    <p><em>During shutdown:</em></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Reduce all demands to absolute minimum</li>
      <li>Quiet, dark, low-stimulation environment</li>
      <li>No expectations for communication or processing</li>
      <li>As much time as needed (hours to days, not minutes)</li>
    </ul>

    <p><em>During meltdown:</em></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Focus on safety only</li>
      <li>Don't try to reason or communicate during the meltdown</li>
      <li>Remove sensory triggers if possible</li>
      <li>Prepare for shutdown phase that typically follows</li>
    </ul>

    <p><em>What doesn't help:</em></p>
    <ul>
      <li>"Just try harder" (increases energy demand)</li>
      <li>"Learn better coping skills" (if the model is right, this is more about energy than psychology)</li>
      <li>"Push through it" (forces deeper depletion and longer recovery)</li>
      <li>Punishment or consequences (doesn't address energy limits)</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Sensory Environments That May Help</h3>

    <p>If the model is right, an autistic brain may take in sensory input at higher resolution—more neurons processing more details of every stimulus. That would mean unpredictable sensory environments aren't just "annoying"—they could be genuinely exhausting at a neural level.</p>

    <p><strong>What makes environments hard:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Fluorescent lighting (flicker that higher-resolution processing may pick up)</li>
      <li>Open floor plans (too many sensory streams to process simultaneously)</li>
      <li>Background noise (distributed processing captures it all, can't easily filter)</li>
      <li>Temperature fluctuations (constant adjustment demands)</li>
      <li>Unpredictable transitions (high prediction error costs)</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>What makes environments supportive:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Controllable lighting:</strong> Dimmable, warm LED lights you control</li>
      <li><strong>Quiet spaces:</strong> Noise-dampening materials, white noise to mask unpredictable sounds</li>
      <li><strong>Consistent temperature:</strong> Stable thermal environment</li>
      <li><strong>Predictable structure:</strong> Visual schedules, advance warning of changes</li>
      <li><strong>Sensory refuge:</strong> A quiet space you can retreat to when approaching overload</li>
      <li><strong>User control:</strong> Ability to adjust lighting, sound, temperature to your needs</li>
    </ul>

    <p>I think of these less as "special accommodations" and more as ergonomics, like having a comfortable chair or good lighting. Everyone works better in the right environment; autistic brains may just need different specifications.</p>

    <h3>School and Work: Playing to Your Strengths</h3>

    <p><strong>The problem with traditional structures:</strong></p>

    <p>Standard school and work environments often demand what I think distributed processing makes hardest:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Constant task-switching (high transition costs)</li>
      <li>Multitasking (concurrent distributed pathways drain energy fast)</li>
      <li>Short bursts on many topics (prevents deep engagement)</li>
      <li>Unpredictable schedules (sustained high prediction error)</li>
      <li>Open floor plans (sensory overload)</li>
      <li>Implicit expectations (forces extra processing to infer unstated rules)</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>What works with distributed architecture:</strong></p>

    <p><em>Deep focus and special interests:</em> When you're deeply engaged with something you care about and understand well, my guess is that your distributed pathways are pulling in the same direction. There are fewer surprises, and less energy is wasted. I don't see this as distraction or obsession—it may be your brain working at its best.</p>

    <ul>
      <li><strong>Schools:</strong> Project-based learning allowing sustained engagement with topics of interest</li>
      <li><strong>Workplaces:</strong> Specialized roles that use strengths like pattern recognition, attention to detail, and systematic thinking</li>
    </ul>

    <p><em>Explicit structure and task breakdown:</em> In distributed processing, I think complex tasks tend to break down into many micro-steps. Explicit breakdowns may help:</p>

    <ul>
      <li>Written instructions with numbered steps</li>
      <li>Checklists making implicit steps explicit</li>
      <li>Clear expectations and deadlines</li>
      <li>Task management tools with defined sub-tasks</li>
    </ul>

    <p>This isn't reducing expectations—it's providing external scaffolding that matches your internal processing.</p>

    <p><em>Minimal task-switching:</em></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Allow sustained focus on one project before switching</li>
      <li>Batch similar tasks together</li>
      <li>Provide transition time between different types of work</li>
      <li>Minimize interruptions and impromptu requests</li>
    </ul>

    <p><em>Predictable patterns:</em></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Consistent schedules and routines</li>
      <li>Advance notice of changes (as much detail as possible)</li>
      <li>Written communication preferred over verbal (allows processing time)</li>
      <li>Clear policies and procedures rather than implicit social rules</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Stimming: Reactive Overflow and Why Suppression Makes Things Worse</h3>

    <p>My picture of stimming is that it happens when the brain has more activation than it can contain internally. Think of it like water overflowing from a full bucket—when distributed activation exceeds what your inhibitory systems can manage, it may spill into the most accessible outlet: motor circuits. On this view, the movement isn't planned or chosen; it emerges when internal activation has nowhere else to go.</p>

    <p><strong>What stimming represents:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Overflow of distributed activation into motor pathways</li>
      <li>Natural consequence of high internal processing load</li>
      <li>Reactive output—not a regulatory tool</li>
      <li>Sign that your brain is managing a lot internally</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>What I'd expect to happen when stimming is suppressed:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>The overflow activation is still there—you just can't express it through movement</li>
      <li>Now you have to actively work to keep it from becoming movement (recruiting control centers)</li>
      <li>This takes extra brain resources and energy on top of the existing load</li>
      <li>If suppression is fear-driven ("I'll be judged"), your stress systems activate, adding more load</li>
      <li>Combined result: possibly a faster path to energy depletion and collapse</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>The shift:</strong> I wouldn't view stimming as something to eliminate. It may be a visible sign of invisible internal processing. If so, suppressing it doesn't reduce the load—it adds to it. That's why I'd favor accommodations that allow stimming, since blocking the outlet seems likely to increase distress without addressing what triggered the overflow.</p>

    <h3>Routines and Predictability: Not Rigidity, Energy Management</h3>

    <p>Preference for routine often gets labeled as "inflexibility" or "rigidity." But from the DPM perspective, it looks more like sensible energy management.</p>

    <p><strong>Why I think unpredictability is so costly:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Your brain may give a lot of weight to sensory input (what's actually happening)</li>
      <li>When reality doesn't match predictions, the error signal may spread through many distributed pathways</li>
      <li>Updating all those pathways would take real energy</li>
      <li>Ongoing uncertainty would mean ongoing surprise—constantly expensive</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Why routines may help:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Minimize prediction errors (reality matches expectations)</li>
      <li>Reduce baseline energy expenditure</li>
      <li>Free up cognitive resources for actual tasks rather than constant prediction updating</li>
      <li>Create stable operating conditions where distributed processing works efficiently</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>What supportive people do:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Provide advance notice of changes with as much detail as possible</li>
      <li>Explain <em>why</em> changes are happening</li>
      <li>Maintain predictable structure wherever possible</li>
      <li>Recognize that flexibility <em>is</em> happening—just at higher internal cost than neurotypical brains</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Therapy I Would Expect to Help</h3>

    <p><strong>Reframing the problem:</strong></p>

    <p>Traditional therapy often frames autistic challenges as psychological problems requiring better coping skills, emotional regulation, or executive function training. But if the model is right, many of these challenges are <em>structural</em>—they come from how the brain is wired, not from psychological deficits.</p>

    <p><strong>Approaches I would expect to help:</strong></p>

    <p><em>Energy budget management:</em></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Learning to recognize your capacity limits</li>
      <li>Identifying what drains energy fastest for you specifically</li>
      <li>Building in recovery time before depletion</li>
      <li>Accepting that your budget is real and valid</li>
    </ul>

    <p><em>Interoceptive awareness:</em></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Learning to notice early signs of approaching overload</li>
      <li>Identifying your specific warning signals (physical sensations, thought patterns, behavioral changes)</li>
      <li>Acting on these signals proactively rather than pushing through</li>
    </ul>

    <p><em>Environmental modification:</em></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Identifying which sensory environments work for you</li>
      <li>Learning to advocate for accommodations</li>
      <li>Creating supportive home and work spaces</li>
      <li>Recognizing you're not "asking for special treatment"—you're optimizing your environment ergonomically</li>
    </ul>

    <p><em>Realistic self-understanding:</em></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Understanding your brain's architecture and how it works</li>
      <li>Recognizing strengths (detailed perception, pattern recognition, detail focus)</li>
      <li>Accepting constraints (energy limits, task-switching costs, prediction error sensitivity) without self-blame</li>
      <li>Building strategies that work <em>with</em> your architecture, not against it</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Approaches I would expect to backfire:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>"Just push through it" (ignores metabolic limits)</li>
      <li>"Learn to be more flexible" (ignores structural costs of unpredictability)</li>
      <li>"Practice eye contact" (increases cognitive load without addressing underlying architecture)</li>
      <li>"Stop stimming" (forces costly suppression of reactive motor cascades without addressing overflow)</li>
      <li>Framing challenges as willpower failures (ignores neural architecture)</li>
    </ul>


    <h3>The Future: Research and New Approaches</h3>

    <p>If this way of thinking holds up, it could point to some new directions for support. None of these has been tested:</p>

    <p><strong>Better assessment:</strong> Instead of just measuring behaviors, look at each person's energy budget, their specific collapse triggers, and the conditions where they work best.</p>

    <p><strong>Personalized accommodations:</strong> If people differ in how their distributed architecture is set up, supports would work best when they match the individual rather than following generic "autism interventions."</p>

    <p><strong>Areas worth researching:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>How the brain's "brake system" (inhibition) works in autism—I'm not recommending any medication, and none has been tested against this model</li>
      <li>Energy management strategies</li>
      <li>Environmental design that suits distributed processing</li>
    </ul>

    <p>I've also written about what would make me more or less confident in this idea, and what I don't yet know how to test, in the paper's section <a href="../hrh-paper#8-what-would-change-my-mind-and-what-i-dont-know-how-to-test">What would change my mind</a>.</p>

    <h3>The Core Message: Different, Not Deficient</h3>

    <p>As I see it, distributed neural architecture isn't broken hierarchical architecture. It's a different configuration with:</p>

    <p><strong>Different strengths:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Higher-resolution perception and cognition</li>
      <li>Exceptional pattern recognition</li>
      <li>Attention to detail others miss</li>
      <li>Deep, systematic understanding of areas of interest</li>
      <li>Accurate memory for details</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Different costs:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Higher energy consumption</li>
      <li>Greater sensitivity to unpredictability</li>
      <li>Difficulty with concurrent demands</li>
      <li>Higher transition costs between tasks</li>
      <li>Need for sensory accommodation</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Different optimal conditions:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Predictable environments</li>
      <li>Deep focus opportunities</li>
      <li>Sensory control</li>
      <li>Explicit structure</li>
      <li>Sustained engagement over rapid switching</li>
    </ul>

    <p>The goal isn't to make autistic brains process like neurotypical brains. It's to create conditions where distributed processing can work well—keeping the costs down while making the most of the strengths.</p>

    <p>This reframing changes a lot:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>From:</strong> "What's wrong that needs fixing?"</li>
      <li><strong>To:</strong> "What does this architecture need to function well?"</li>
    </ul>

    <ul>
      <li><strong>From:</strong> "Learn to act more normal"</li>
      <li><strong>To:</strong> "Let's design environments and supports that work with how your brain actually works"</li>
    </ul>

    <ul>
      <li><strong>From:</strong> "You need to try harder"</li>
      <li><strong>To:</strong> "Let's identify your energy budget and work within it"</li>
    </ul>

    <ul>
      <li><strong>From:</strong> "Special accommodations for disability"</li>
      <li><strong>To:</strong> "Ergonomic optimization for a different processing architecture"</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Seeing autism through the DPM means recognizing autistic cognition as valid and coherent—a different way of processing information with its own logic, strengths, and needs. The path forward I'd hope for isn't normalization. It's a world where distributed processing can thrive.</p>
